The Miraculous Night

During a time of immense personal sorrow — the Year of Sorrow (ʿĀm al-Ḥuzn) — Allah honoured the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ with an ascension unlike any other in human history. He was taken from Mecca to Jerusalem, and from there ascended through the layers of the universe, piercing the veils of the unseen to reach the Divine Presence.

"Exalted is He who took His Servant by night from al-Masjid al-Ḥarām to al-Masjid al-Aqṣā, whose surroundings We have blessed, to show him of Our signs." — Sūrat al-Isrāʾ (17:1)

What follows draws together the earthly Night Journey (al-Isrāʾ), the heavenly Ascent (al-Miʿrāj), the sacred steed Buraq who bore the Prophet ﷺ across both, and a comparison with Dante's later journey through the same celestial architecture — the threshold-crossing that gives the whole compendium its first and highest exhibit.

— The Mount of Prophets —

Buraq

البُراق · "The Lightning"
The luminous celestial steed from Paradise
Islamic Tradition Brought from Paradise Bearer: Muḥammad ﷺ
Buraq — the celestial steed, in the Timurid/Safavid miniature tradition.

Derived from the Arabic word for "lightning," Buraq is a luminous celestial creature from Paradise. The hadith tradition describes it in remarkably consistent detail:

  • A white creature from Paradise, smaller than a mule and larger than a donkey.
  • Possessed of wings on its sides to propel its incredible speed.
  • With every single stride, it placed its hoof at the furthest limit of its vision.
  • Initially hesitant out of awe, it stood completely still when Gabriel informed it that the Prophet ﷺ was to mount it.
  • Upon reaching Jerusalem, it was tethered to the ancient ring used by all previous prophets at the gate of al-Aqṣā.
"I was brought al-Buraq, a white animal, smaller than a mule and larger than a donkey. He would place his hoof at the farthest boundary of his sight." — Saḥīḥ Muslim, Kitāb al-Īmān, 162

The Earthly Night Journey — A Deep Study

Al-Isrāʾ

الإسراء

Before the ascent to heaven, there was an earthly journey. From the Sacred Mosque in Mecca to the Farthest Mosque in Jerusalem — a journey of some 1,200 kilometres completed in part of a single night, with five stops, four prophetic sites, and a gathering that had never occurred before in history.

سُبْحَانَ الَّذِي أَسْرَىٰ بِعَبْدِهِ لَيْلًا مِّنَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ إِلَى الْمَسْجِدِ الْأَقْصَى الَّذِي بَارَكْنَا حَوْلَهُ لِنُرِيَهُ مِنْ آيَاتِنَا ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْبَصِيرُ

"Glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque — whose surroundings We have blessed — so that We may show him some of Our signs. Indeed, He alone is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing." (Sūrat al-Isrāʾ · 17:1)

سُبْحَانَ · Subḥāna

Glory — the Qurʾān opens the verse not with history but with praise, marking the journey as an act of divine transcendence before any detail is given.

لَيْلًا · Laylan

By night — the indefinite noun signals a part of the night only. The entire journey, there and back, fit inside a single night's portion.

عَبْدِهِ · ʿAbdihi

His servant — the word ʿabd is the highest title in this context, used to confirm the Prophet's physical, bodily presence during the journey.

I. The Divine Command — Who Ordered the Journey?

The journey was not requested, proposed, or planned by any human or angel. The Qurʾān makes the initiator clear in the first word: Subḥāna alladhī asrā — "Glory be to the One Who caused to travel." Allah initiated, commanded, and executed this miracle directly. The verb asrā (to cause to journey by night) is causative — Allah is the sole cause.

The year was the 11th year of prophethood — approximately 620–621 CE — and it had been the most devastating year of the Prophet's ﷺ life. The scholars call it ʿĀm al-Ḥuzn, the Year of Sorrow. His beloved wife Khadījah RA — the first believer — had died. His uncle Abū Ṭālib, his protector against the Quraysh, had died within weeks of her. The people of Ṭāʾif had chased him from their city with stone-throwers, children mocking him in the streets.

The Isrāʾ wal-Miʿrāj was Allah's answer to that grief. Not a consolation — a revelation. Not words of comfort — a journey to the Divine Presence itself. The One Who had seen every stone that hit him, every insult, every loss, said: Come. Let Me show you My signs.

II. The Preparation — Jibrīl's Arrival

The Prophet ﷺ was resting in the Ḥijr — the semi-circular enclosure beside the Kaʿbah — when the Angel Jibrīl descended. He came with Mīkāʾīl. Together they carried the Prophet to the Well of Zamzam and performed the Shaqq al-Ṣadr — the Opening of the Chest. This had happened once before, in the Prophet's childhood; now it was repeated before the greatest journey in human history.

Step 1

The Chest Opened

Jibrīl opened the Prophet's chest from throat to belly — without pain, without wound (Saḥīḥ Muslim 162).

Step 2

The Heart Removed

The Prophet's heart was taken out and placed in a golden tray, gleaming with light.

Step 3

Washed with Zamzam

The heart was washed clean — purified, the scholars say, of every trace of doubt, forgetfulness, and heedlessness.

Step 4

The Two Bowls

Two golden bowls were brought — one of wisdom (ḥikmah), one of faith (īmān). Both were poured into the heart, which was returned and the chest sealed.

The Isrāʾ — Mecca to Jerusalem. Safavid miniature tradition · the Prophet ﷺ rides Buraq through the celestial night, accompanied by Jibrīl.

III. The Five Stations of the Night

Jibrīl did not simply fly from Mecca to Jerusalem in a straight line. At four points along the route he halted Buraq, turned to the Prophet ﷺ, and said: "Dismount and pray." Each stop was a monument of prophetic history. Each prayer was a consecration. The route was not geographical — it was theological.

01 · Prophecy Revealed

Yathrib — The Future Madinah

يَثْرِب / طَيْبَة

The Prophet ﷺ prayed two rakʿahs here. Jibrīl then asked: "Do you know where you prayed? You prayed in Ṭaybah — which will be the place of your emigration." He had no plan yet to emigrate; within two years he would leave Mecca forever for this very soil. (Sunan al-Nasāʾī 450)

02

Madyan — Land of Mūsā & Shuʿayb

مَدْيَن

He prayed at the tree of Mūsā — where Moses had rested as a destitute fugitive after fleeing Egypt, crying: "My Lord, I am in need of whatever good You send down to me." (Qurʾān 28:24) Moses' helplessness and Muhammad's elevation mirror each other across the centuries.

03

Mount Sinai — Ṭūr Sīnāʾ

طُور سِيْنَاء

He prayed where Allah had spoken directly to Mūsā and the Torah was revealed. The Qurʾān swears an oath by this mountain (95:1–2). To pray here was to stand at the axis of the Abrahamic covenant — at the very peak where revelation had once thundered down.

04

Bayt Laḥm — Bethlehem

بَيْتُ لَحْم

The final stop before Jerusalem — the birthplace of Prophet ʿĪsā ﷺ (Jesus). The ground of the Nativity, consecrated now by the footstep and prayer of the Last Prophet.

05 · The Summit

Al-Masjid al-Aqṣā — Jerusalem

الْمَسْجِدُ الأَقْصَى

Buraq was tethered at the ancient ring all prophets' mounts had used. Jibrīl offered two vessels — milk and wine; the Prophet ﷺ chose the milk, the fiṭra. Then he led every prophet from Ādam to ʿĪsā in prayer. (Qurʾān 17:1 · Saḥīḥ Muslim · Saḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

Al-Masjid al-Aqṣā — the Farthest Mosque · Timurid miniature.

IV. Imam of All the Prophets

When the Prophet ﷺ entered al-Masjid al-Aqṣā, he found it filled with luminous figures — the assembled prophets of all history, from Ādam ﷺ to ʿĪsā ﷺ. This is narrated explicitly in Saḥīḥ Muslim (172). Then the iqāmah was called, and every prophet — Ibrāhīm the friend of Allah, Mūsā who spoke with Allah, ʿĪsā created without a father — stood in rows. And Muḥammad ﷺ stepped forward to lead them. Imām al-Anbiyāʾ — the Imam of the Prophets.

"Two vessels were brought to me — one of wine and one of milk. I chose the milk. Gabriel said: 'You have chosen the fiṭra (the natural disposition). Had you chosen wine, your community would have gone astray.'" — Saḥīḥ al-Bukhārī · Saḥīḥ Muslim
The Isrāʾ — What It Reveals. The Night Journey was not a shortcut to Jerusalem. It was a theological curriculum — four stops, four prophetic legacies, four prayers on four sacred grounds — before the summit at al-Aqṣā. Every footstep said: the one who prays here continues the work of all who came before. Then the Miʿrāj began.

The Sacred Steed — A Deep Study

The World of Buraq

Of all the miraculous elements of the Night Journey, none has captured the Islamic imagination more profoundly than Buraq — the lightning-steed from Paradise who carried the Prophet ﷺ across the horizons of creation.

Buraq in the Gardens of Paradise — Persian miniature tradition.

I. Born of Lightning

The name al-Burāq derives from the Arabic root بَرَقَ (baraqa): to flash, to gleam, to radiate like lightning. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, citing the zoologist al-Damīrī (d. 1405), notes no evidence the word existed in Arabic before Islam — it may have been received from Jibrīl in the same breath as the journey itself.

Buraq is not an earthly animal. The hadith tradition is unanimous: it is a celestial creature from the unseen realm, brought from Paradise, already saddled and awaiting its rider — belonging to an order of creation not subject to biological time. Ibn ʿAbbās reported it came bearing an emerald saddle, pearl reins, and turquoise stirrups.

The Body

أَبْيَضُ طَوِيلٌ

White, tall, long — larger than a donkey, smaller than a mule, white as moonlight (Saḥīḥ Muslim 162).

The Stride

يَضَعُ حَافِرَهُ عِنْدَ مُنْتَهَى طَرْفِهِ

Its hoof falls where the eye ends — each stride covered the distance to the furthest visible point on the horizon.

The Face

وَجْهُهُ كَوَجْهِ الإِنْسَانِ

A serene human face — the canonical iconographic tradition across Timurid, Safavid, and Ottoman painting.

The Wings

جَنَاحَانِ فِي فَخِذَيْهِ

Two wings on its thighs — enabling a galloping stride while airborne; the scholar al-Mālikī adds they "lent strength to its legs."

The Ears

طَوِيلُ الأُذُنَيْنِ

Long ears, consistently noted across the descriptions and visible in the Timurid Miʿrāj-nāma (1436, Paris).

The Tail & Saddle

سَرْجٌ مِنْ زُمُرُّدٍ

A saddle of emerald — later accounts add a peacock-like tail, silk mane, pearl reins; its legs lengthening on slopes to stay perfectly balanced.

Buraq tethered at al-Masjid al-Aqṣā — Ottoman miniature tradition.

II. What Buraq Said

The canonical collections do not record Buraq speaking in words — but they record its behaviour, and Gabriel's extraordinary speech to it, which implies Buraq understood every word. When brought to the Prophet ﷺ, saddled and bridled, it shied away. Gabriel said: "Is it from Muhammad that you do this? By Allah, no one more honoured before Allah has ever ridden you." Then it became ashamed and sweated. (Musnad Aḥmad 13546)

Buraq had carried Ibrāhīm ﷺ before this night (Sunan Ibn Mājah). At Jerusalem the Prophet ﷺ tethered it to "the ring to which the prophets used to tether their mounts" (Saḥīḥ Muslim) — a relic of prophetic history, a hinge between all the ages.

"Then I tethered it to the ring to which the prophets used to tether [their mounts]." — Saḥīḥ Muslim · Kitāb al-Īmān

III. The Divine Horse Across Civilisations

A white celestial horse that bridges the earthly and the divine is among the most universal archetypes in human spiritual tradition. From Greece to India, from Scandinavia to the Turkic steppes, the sacred steed appears again and again — the same archetype the wider compendium gathers in full.

The divine celestial horse — an archetype across world traditions.

Pegasus

Greek

Born from the blood of Medusa, captured with Athena's golden bridle. His name may derive from the Luwian pihassas, "lightning." Like Buraq, pure white and heaven-dwelling.

Uchaiśravas

Hindu Vedic

The seven-headed snow-white horse churned from the cosmic ocean, mount of Indra, god of thunder. Both emerge from a cosmic event rather than natural birth.

Sleipnir

Norse

Odin's eight-legged steed, able to traverse Hel, Midgard, and Asgard. Both transcend the natural order with an anomalous form fitted for inter-realm travel.

Kanthaka

Buddhist

The white horse who bore Prince Siddhārtha on the Great Departure, then died of grief and was reborn in heaven. A pure white horse enabling a single transformative night journey.

Tulpar

Turkic

The winged divine horse of Central Asian epic — granted only to the worthy hero. The same cultural sphere produced the painters who first visualised Buraq.

Haizum

Islamic

The horse of Jibrīl himself, who descended on it at Badr with a flaming face. The mount of the Archangel; Buraq, the mount of the Prophet.

The Thread of Lightning. Buraq's name means lightning. Pegasus may mean lightning in Luwian. Uchaiśravas belongs to Indra, god of thunder. Sleipnir runs with Odin across storm-wracked skies. The Islamic tradition does not merely share this archetype — it fulfils it: where every other sacred horse carries a god or hero to a battle or an adventure, Buraq carries a prophet to the Divine Presence itself, to the point where even Gabriel must stop and only the Beloved of Allah proceeds further.

The Ascension — Al-Miʿrāj

Through the Seven Heavens

Guided by Gabriel, the Prophet ﷺ ascended through the celestial realms, greeted at each by the greatest messengers of the past.

The ascent through the celestial realms.
The Imam of Prophets — in the sacred precinct of al-Aqṣā, every prophet from Ādam to ʿĪsā assembled, and the Prophet ﷺ led them in prayer.
Heaven 1

Ādam ﷺ

The father of humanity. He welcomed the Prophet warmly, acknowledging his righteous son.

Heaven 2

ʿĪsā & Yaḥyā ﷺ

The maternal cousins, Jesus and John the Baptist, who greeted him with joy.

Heaven 3

Yūsuf ﷺ

Joseph, who was granted half of all beauty in existence.

Heaven 4

Idrīs ﷺ

Enoch, whom Allah had raised to a lofty, exalted station.

Heaven 5

Hārūn ﷺ

Aaron, the beloved elder brother of Moses.

Heaven 6

Mūsā ﷺ

Moses, who wept knowing the Prophet's followers would outnumber his own in Paradise.

Heaven 7

Ibrāhīm ﷺ

Abraham, leaning against al-Bayt al-Maʿmūr, the Heavenly House.

Sidrat al-Muntahā — the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary.

Wonders of the Miʿrāj

Beyond the seventh heaven, realities incomprehensible to the mortal mind were revealed:

Sidrat al-Muntahā — the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary, covered in golden moths, marking the end of all creation, beyond which neither angel nor prophet had passed.

Al-Bayt al-Maʿmūr — the Heavenly House directly above the Kaʿbah, which 70,000 angels enter daily to worship and never return.

Paradise & Hellfire — the vast beauty of Paradise with its four rivers, and Mālik, the keeper of Hellfire, who never smiles.

❦ ❦ ❦

The Greatest Gift

In the divine presence, Allah ordained fifty daily prayers for the believers. As the Prophet descended, Mūsā advised him to return and ask for a reduction, knowing humanity's weakness. He returned repeatedly, each time receiving a reduction of ten, until five remained. When Mūsā urged him once more, the Prophet ﷺ replied:

"I have asked my Lord until I am too shy. I accept and submit." — The Night of Ascent

Thus Allah declared that these five prayers would carry the reward of the original fifty. The ṣalāh became the believer's personal Miʿrāj — a direct connection to the Divine.

Comparative Sacred Literature

Two Journeys Through the Heavens

The Isrāʾ wal-Miʿrāj and Dante Alighieri's Divina Commedia (1308–1320) stand as two of history's most extraordinary accounts of a soul ascending through the celestial realms — a guide, a vertical ascent through layered heavens, encounters with the great souls of the past, and a final overwhelming vision of the Divine.

The Spanish scholar Miguel Asín Palacios caused an international sensation with his 1919 work La Escatología Musulmana en la Divina Comedia, arguing that Dante had access to Islamic eschatological literature — specifically the Liber Scale Machometi (the Book of the Ladder of Muhammad), an Arabic account of the Miʿrāj translated into Latin and French in 1264, decades before Dante wrote his Comedy.

The Islamic Account

فَجَاءَ جِبْرِيلُ فَفَرَجَ صَدْرِي ثُمَّ غَسَلَهُ بِمَاءِ زَمْزَمَ ثُمَّ جَاءَ بِطَسْتٍ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ مُمْتَلِئٍ حِكْمَةً وَإِيمَانًا

"Gabriel came and opened my chest, then washed it with Zamzam water, then brought a golden basin filled with wisdom and faith." — Saḥīḥ al-Bukhārī

Dante Alighieri

"La gloria di colui che tutto move / per l'universo penetra, e risplende / in una parte più e meno altrove."

"The glory of Him who moves all things penetrates through the universe and shines in one part more, and in another less." — Paradiso · Canto I

The Guides — Jibrīl & Virgil · Beatrice

Jibrīl (Gabriel) ﷺ

The Archangel who brought the Qurʾān guides the Prophet ﷺ through every heaven, announcing at each gate: "This is Muhammad, and I have been sent with him." Yet even Gabriel cannot pass beyond Sidrat al-Muntahā — "This is the limit of what my Lord has commanded me" (al-Mustadrak) — just as Virgil cannot enter Paradise.

Virgil & Beatrice

Dante needs two guides. Virgil — Human Reason — leads him through Hell and Purgatory but cannot enter Paradise, an unbaptised pagan. At the summit of Purgatory, Beatrice — Divine Theology and Grace — takes over. The handoff from reason to faith mirrors the Prophet's passage from the knowable world into the realm beyond all angels.

The Encounter with Adam

The First Heaven · Islamic Tradition

فَإِذَا رَجُلٌ جَالِسٌ، وَعَلَى يَمِينِهِ أَسْوِدَةٌ وَعَلَى شِمَالِهِ أَسْوِدَةٌ، إِذَا نَظَرَ قِبَلَ يَمِينِهِ ضَحِكَ، وَإِذَا نَظَرَ قِبَلَ شِمَالِهِ بَكَى

"There sat a man, shadows on his right and left. When he looked right he laughed; when he looked left he wept. Gabriel said: This is Adam, and the shadows are the souls of his children." (Saḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3887) The Islamic Adam carries all of human history simultaneously — not as guilt, but as love.

The Empyrean · Paradiso Canto XXVI

"Or, figliuol mio, non il gustar del legno / fu per sé la cagion di tanto essilio, / ma solamente il trapassar del segno."

"Know, my son, that the tasting of the tree was not in itself the cause of so long an exile, but solely the transgression of the sign." Dante's Adam is philosophical and retrospective — he speaks not of weeping for his children but of disobedience as a principle.

Dante's Paradiso — the celestial spheres.
Dante's Inferno — the circles of Hell.
Jahannam & the Inferno. During the Miʿrāj the Prophet ﷺ was shown Hellfire — a vast abyss guarded by Mālik, who has never smiled, with seven gates and nineteen angels. Dante's Inferno is an architecture of nine descending circles, each calibrated to the sin by contrapasso. Above its gate: "Per me si va ne la città dolente" — "Through me the way into the suffering city." Both traditions agree: the vision of it changes everything.
"Amor che move il sole e l'altre stelle." — "The love that moves the sun and the other stars." — Paradiso, Canto XXXIII (final line)

The Journey Within

The Miʿrāj reminds us that when earthly doors close, the heavens open. It invites us to elevate our hearts in prayer, to step away from the worldly briefly, and to seek proximity to the Divine.

May Allah grant us the devotion to guard the gift of prayer, and illuminate our hearts with the light of this miraculous night. Ameen.
