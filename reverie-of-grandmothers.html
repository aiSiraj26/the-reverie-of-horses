On the Grandmother Who Carries

The Reverie of Horses gathered ten celestial steeds (and seventeen further carriers in the library expansion) from the mythological traditions of the human imagination. The horses were carriers of transformation: they bore single heroes across single thresholds, often into transcendence, sometimes into death, always into a register the hero could not have reached alone. The structural identity ran clean through the canon. Buraq carried the Prophet ﷺ through the seven heavens. Pegasus carried Bellerophon above the Chimera. Rakhsh chose Rostam and stayed with him through every labor and every defeat until they died together in the same pit. The pattern was the pattern of the heroic individual borne by the substantive carrier toward the moment that completes him.

The Reverie of Grandmothers gathers the same structural pattern in a different and more demanding register. Where the horses are mythological, the grandmothers are historical. Where the horses bore single heroes, the grandmothers have borne villages, towns, and cities. Where the horses carried for one luminous night, the grandmothers have carried for decades. Where the hero falls when his horse is taken from him (Bellerophon goes lame and blind; Pegasus ascends alone), the grandmother continues. She continues when the institutions fail, when the men leave or are killed, when the weather changes, when the police arrive, when the children are taken. She continues, and the continuing itself is the carrying.

This volume gathers verified figures. Each exhibit names a grandmother (or in some cases a small collective of grandmothers) whose transformation of a community is documented in the public record — by court testimony, by Nobel commendation, by peer-reviewed scholarship, by photographs, by the still-living memories of those whose lives were transformed. The verification is part of the methodology. The Reverie of Horses had to gather what tradition preserved; the Reverie of Grandmothers gathers what witness preserved. Witness is harder to falsify and harder to dismiss.

◆

Three structural differences between the horse and the grandmother as carrier deserve to be named at the outset, because they shape every exhibit that follows.

The first: the grandmother carries without being chosen. The horse waits to be tamed; Bucephalus waits for the boy who can see the shadow; Rakhsh waits for the rider who will not buckle his back. The carrier-and-carried relationship in the horse register is constituted by an act of mutual recognition between two substantive figures, and the constitution is the substance of the exhibit. The grandmother does not wait. She carries whoever needs carrying. The granddaughter who is brought to her after the parents have been killed; the village whose chief has died; the community whose institutions have collapsed; the migrants on the train tracks who arrive hungry. The grandmother's carrying is not constituted by mutual recognition; it is constituted by the carried's need and the grandmother's presence. The act of recognition runs only one direction. She sees. The carried does not have to see in return — and often does not, until much later, or sometimes never.

The second: the grandmother carries for decades. The horse's carrying is episodic — the Night Journey, the Chimera, the road to Hel. The grandmother's carrying is durative. Wangari Maathai planted trees with rural Kenyan women for thirty-four years between the founding of the Green Belt Movement and her death. The Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo have walked the plaza every Thursday afternoon since April 1977 — over forty-eight years as of this writing, and they continue. Gaura Devi's intervention at Reni was a single afternoon, but the Chipko movement she initiated has carried the Garhwal forests for fifty years. The horse carries for the constitutive moment; the grandmother carries for the constitutive life. The two timescales are different orders of magnitude.

The third: the grandmother carries communities, not individuals. The hero is borne alone — Buraq's stride lands at the farthest point his eye can see, but only the Prophet ﷺ rides upon his back. The grandmother is borne not at all; she does the bearing, and what she bears is plural. Her village. Her town. The thirty thousand disappeared. The fifty-one million trees. The hundreds of stolen grandchildren. The Pioneer Soulmate Method's fifth aspect — looking into the dark and sensing the destination of the soul — is, when applied to the grandmother register, the recognition that the soul whose destination she senses is rarely her own. She senses the soul-direction of the community. She carries the community toward what the community could not, at the moment of carrying, see for itself.

◆

There is a methodological consequence. The Pioneer Soulmate Method has, in its formulation by way of the horse-tradition, sometimes been read as an individualistic methodology — the executive being carried by his coach, the leader being carried by his mentor, the hero being carried by the carrier whose substance enables his ascent. The reading is not wrong but it is incomplete. The grandmother register restores the missing dimension. The carrier-and-carried relationship operates equally well, and perhaps more substantively, at the scale of the community. The temenos — the bounded developmental ground — is not only the executive coaching engagement. It is also the kitchen at five in the morning where the grandmother begins the day's bread. It is the Plaza de Mayo on Thursday afternoon. It is the forest path where the women walked out to meet the loggers. The bounded developmental ground is wherever the carrier sustains the carrying without flinching, for as long as the carrying is required.

This volume opens with three exhibits gathered as its canon. The three are chosen for their geographical and registral range: Wangari Maathai of Kenya (environmental and political transformation across a continent), Estela de Carlotto and the Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo of Argentina (the recovery of stolen children and the indictment of a dictatorship), and Gaura Devi of Reni in the Garhwal Himalayas (a single afternoon that began the modern Indian environmental movement). They share three features: the carrying was sustained for decades, the verification is unimpeachable, and the transformation is measurable in numbers — trees planted, grandchildren returned, hectares of forest preserved — that the documentary record can produce in evidence.

From that canon the volume opens outward into eleven further exhibits, gathered here at the same depth and to the same standard of verification: the Solar Grandmothers of Barefoot College, Grace Lee Boggs of Detroit, Las Patronas of Veracruz, Maggie Kuhn and the Gray Panthers, Doris "Granny D" Haddock of New Hampshire, the International Council of Thirteen Indigenous Grandmothers, Anna Mary Robertson Moses, Tulasi Gowda of Karnataka, Ela Bhatt and SEWA, the Mothers of Soacha, and Setsuko Thurlow of Hiroshima. The library is open still; every village has at least one carrier, and the gathering, by you and by whatever carriers come after you, is without end.

— Exhibit I —

Wangari Maathai

1940–2011 · Born Nyeri, Central Province · Died Nairobi
The Grandmother of the Green Belt Movement
Kenya 1977–2011 Community: rural Kenyan women & the forests of East Africa Transformation: environmental restoration as political and feminist work
Wangari Maathai, founder of the Green Belt Movement and the first African woman to receive the Nobel Peace Prize. Wikimedia Commons · CC BY.

Wangari Muta Maathai was born on the first of April, 1940, in the village of Ihithe in the Tetu division of Nyeri District, in the Central Province of what was then the British Kenya Colony. She was the third of six children born to Muta and Wanjirū Maathai of the Kikuyu people, the largest ethnic community of central Kenya. Her father worked as a farmhand on a settler-owned coffee estate; her mother worked the family's land in the village. The family was, by the measure of their place and time, ordinary. Nothing in the conditions of her birth suggested that she would become the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a doctorate, the first African woman to receive the Nobel Peace Prize, and the founder of one of the most consequential environmental movements of the twentieth century.

The transformation that her life produced began, in its public form, on the fifth of June, 1977 — World Environment Day — when Maathai, then a thirty-seven-year-old lecturer in veterinary anatomy at the University of Nairobi and the head of the National Council of Women of Kenya, planted seven trees in a public ceremony at the Kamukunji Park in Nairobi to commemorate Kenyan environmental heroes. The ceremony might have remained symbolic. It did not, because Maathai understood — in a way that the international environmental movement of her time did not yet understand — that the planting of trees was, in the rural Kenyan context, an act of women's labor whose mobilization could simultaneously address deforestation, soil erosion, fuelwood shortage, and the political and economic disenfranchisement of rural women. The act of planting was, in her formulation, simultaneously ecological, feminist, and democratic.

"When we plant trees, we plant the seeds of peace and the seeds of hope." — Wangari Maathai, Nobel Lecture, Oslo, 10 December 2004

From the founding ceremony at Kamukunji, the Green Belt Movement grew through the late 1970s and 1980s into a network of rural women's tree-planting groups across Kenya. The model was simple and uncompromisingly practical. A village women's group would identify a degraded area — a denuded hillside, an eroded riverbank, a community forest that had been cleared for fuelwood — and would establish a tree nursery. The women would grow seedlings of indigenous species (Maathai was insistent that the species be indigenous; she resisted the planting of fast-growing exotics that European foresters often preferred) until the seedlings were strong enough to transplant. The transplantation was a community event. The women would be paid a small sum for each tree that survived the first six months. The money — small in absolute terms but substantial in the context of rural Kenyan women's cash incomes — was the material substance through which the women became the agents of their own economic empowerment. By the time of Maathai's death in 2011, the Green Belt Movement had planted, by its own count, over fifty-one million trees across Kenya.

The fifty-one million is, however, only the most measurable dimension of the transformation. The deeper transformation was political. The Moi regime — Daniel arap Moi was President of Kenya from 1978 to 2002 — recognized that Maathai's organization of rural women into action-capable groups was a threat to the regime's monopoly on community mobilization. The regime responded with the standard apparatus of authoritarian harassment. Maathai was arrested repeatedly. She was beaten by police during a 1992 protest against the regime's plans to construct a sixty-story tower in Uhuru Park (which had been public green space). She was vilified in the state press as an unmarried mother of three and a Western-influenced agitator (she had divorced her husband Mwangi Mathai in 1979, after he charged her, in the divorce proceedings, with being "too strong-minded for a woman" and "impossible to control"). She was, in 1991, jailed for treason after publicly criticizing the regime's land-grabbing policies. Through all of it she continued to organize. The Green Belt Movement continued to plant trees. The rural women continued to come to the meetings.

The Pioneer Soulmate Method's fourth aspect — thriving in chaos and finding beauty in difficulty — is, in the Maathai exhibit, the documented historical fact of how an ordinary Kikuyu woman from a coffee-estate family carried, across thirty-four years of escalating pressure, the substantive practice of replanting a country. She was, the women of the movement called her, Mami — mother. By the time of her death, the rural women who had been forty when they first joined the Green Belt Movement were grandmothers in their seventies, and their daughters and granddaughters had taken up the work alongside them. The grandmother who carried the movement had, in carrying it, taught a generation of grandmothers to carry it after her.

The Nobel Peace Prize was awarded to Maathai on the tenth of December, 2004. She was the first African woman to receive it, and the first environmentalist of any continent. The award citation read, in part, that she "thinks globally and acts locally," and that her contribution "to sustainable development, democracy and peace" was the substance for which the Committee recognized her. The Norwegian Nobel Committee's chairman, Ole Danbolt Mjøs, said in his presentation speech that Maathai had been chosen because the Committee had "redefined peace to include environmental issues," and that her work demonstrated that the connection between environment, peace, democracy, and women's rights was "organic and inseparable." Maathai accepted the award wearing a traditional Kikuyu green dress. She arrived at the Oslo City Hall to deliver her Nobel Lecture and began by greeting the Norwegian people in Kikuyu. The Norwegians, baffled by the unfamiliar tones, listened in silence.

The methodology she carried into her work was, in its essence, the carrier methodology that the Pioneer Soulmate framework would later formalize. Maathai met rural women where they were — in their villages, in their gardens, at the wells where they gathered water in the dawn — and offered them not a doctrine but a practice. The practice was the seedling. The seedling required water; the water required the protection of the spring; the spring required the trees; the trees required the women. The whole cosmology was visible in a single nursery bed of fifty seedlings. The carrier did not need to explain that she was carrying. She bent down beside the woman with the trowel, and she demonstrated the depth at which the root ball would survive.

The transformation that her carrying produced is measurable along multiple axes. Environmental: fifty-one million trees, approximately two hundred and fifty hectares of restored watershed forest by the time of her death, demonstrable reductions in soil erosion in the watersheds where the movement operated, the establishment of indigenous tree nurseries as ongoing economic enterprises in over six thousand Kenyan villages. Political: the Green Belt Movement was a leading force in the democratic mobilization that ended the Moi regime in 2002; Maathai herself served as Assistant Minister for Environment, Natural Resources and Wildlife in the post-Moi government from 2003 to 2005. Feminist: the model of women-as-organized-environmental-labor that Maathai pioneered has been replicated, with varying degrees of fidelity to her original principles, in environmental movements across the Global South. Personal: the rural women whose lives were transformed by participation in the movement — most of whom remain anonymous in the historical record — number in the hundreds of thousands.

Maathai died of complications from ovarian cancer in Nairobi on the twenty-fifth of September, 2011, at the age of seventy-one. Her body was cremated, at her instruction, rather than buried; she had been clear, in her later years, that she did not wish her death to require the felling of trees for a coffin. The seedling nurseries continue. The Green Belt Movement, under the leadership of subsequent generations including her daughter Wanjira, continues. The trees she planted continue. The carrier is gone; the carrying continues; the substance of what she carried has become the substance of what the carried can now carry forward. The exhibit gathers her as the first Grandmother of this compendium because the methodology she enacted is the methodology the compendium attempts to articulate, and because her enactment is, by every measure of evidence the documentary record can produce, verified.

— Exhibit II —

Estela de Carlotto & Las Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo

1930– · Founded the organization 1977
The Grandmothers Who Found the Stolen Children
Argentina 1977–present (48 years and counting) Community: the families of the disappeared & 130+ recovered grandchildren Transformation: the documentary recovery of stolen identities and the indictment of a dictatorship
Estela de Carlotto, president of the Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo, who recovered her own stolen grandson after thirty-six years. Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA (Argentine government / press archives).

On the twenty-fourth of March, 1976, the Argentine armed forces under the command of General Jorge Rafael Videla overthrew the government of Isabel Perón and established what the junta itself named the Proceso de Reorganización Nacional — the Process of National Reorganization. The Proceso lasted seven years and seven months, until the fall of the junta's last president Reynaldo Bignone on the tenth of December, 1983. During those years, the Argentine state security apparatus disappeared — secuestró, in the Spanish that has since become the vocabulary of every post-authoritarian transition in Latin America — approximately thirty thousand people. Many were tortured; most were killed; their bodies were, in many cases, thrown into the Río de la Plata or the South Atlantic from military aircraft in what came to be called the vuelos de la muerte, the flights of death.

Among the disappeared were approximately five hundred pregnant women. The military regime's ideology held that the children of subversives could be redeemed if separated from their biological parents and given to military families willing to raise them in proper Catholic-nationalist conditions. The pregnant women were kept alive at the secret detention centers until they gave birth. They were then killed. The babies were taken. The babies were given to military families — sometimes the families of the very officers who had supervised the mothers' torture — and were raised under false identities, often without ever knowing they had been adopted under such circumstances. The state machinery of the appropriation was extensive: forged birth certificates, forged adoption papers, complicit doctors and nurses at the regime-controlled hospitals, complicit judges in the family courts. The forgery was, in many cases, so professional that the appropriated grandchildren could not have suspected, on the documentary evidence alone, that they were not the biological children of the families who raised them.

"They cannot give us back the children we have lost. But they can give us back the grandchildren they have stolen. And so we walk, every Thursday, until they are returned." — Estela de Carlotto, on the fortieth anniversary of the organization, 2017

Estela Barnes de Carlotto was born on the twenty-second of October, 1930, in Buenos Aires. She was, in 1976, a primary-school principal in the city of La Plata, the wife of a chemistry teacher, and the mother of four children. Her eldest daughter Laura, twenty-two years old and three months pregnant, was disappeared by the military on the twenty-sixth of November, 1977. Laura was held at the secret detention center known as La Cacha, in the outskirts of La Plata. She gave birth on the twenty-sixth of June, 1978, to a son the regime's records would later confirm she had named Guido. Laura was killed two months later, in August 1978, her body returned to her mother by the regime in a marked grave (which was unusual, and which the Carlotto family interpreted as the regime's way of indicating, by giving her body back, that she had not been one of the political-prisoner cases the regime intended to deny). Guido, the newborn grandson, was not returned. He was given to a family with military connections in the province of Buenos Aires and raised under another name. Estela de Carlotto did not know whether he had survived. The not-knowing lasted thirty-six years.

Las Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo was founded in October 1977, as the regime's appropriation of grandchildren became apparent to the women whose daughters had disappeared. The founding group of twelve grandmothers gathered in the apartment of Alicia Zubasnabar de De la Cuadra ("Licha"), who had been searching for her pregnant daughter Elena since the previous year. The grandmothers' initial goal was practical: find the grandchildren who had been born in captivity. The methodology was constructed from nothing. There was no precedent. There were no databases. The forgery of the appropriation was professional. The grandmothers were, in the public perception of the regime that controlled Argentina, hysterical, unbalanced, the loud mothers and grandmothers of subversives, to be ignored where possible and harassed where necessary.

They were not ignored. Three of the founding grandmothers — Esther Ballestrino de Careaga, María Ponce de Bianco, and Azucena Villaflor de Vicenti — were themselves disappeared by the regime in December 1977, having been infiltrated by the navy officer Alfredo Astiz, who attended their meetings posing as the brother of a disappeared person. The three women were taken to the Escuela de Mecánica de la Armada (the ESMA, the Navy Mechanics School, which functioned as the regime's principal Buenos Aires torture center) and thrown alive from a military aircraft into the South Atlantic during one of the vuelos de la muerte. Las Abuelas continued.

The methodology the surviving grandmothers developed over the subsequent decades is one of the most extraordinary inventions in the history of human-rights documentation. The grandmothers, working at first with the support of the few Argentine geneticists who would risk their careers to assist them, developed in the early 1980s the conceptual basis of what came to be called the índice de abuelidad — the grandparentage index. The principle was elegant. Conventional paternity testing required samples from both biological parents. The grandmothers did not have the biological parents (the parents had been killed). What they did have was themselves — and the genetic material of the grandparents on both sides. By analyzing the mitochondrial DNA passed down the maternal line, and the Y-chromosome DNA passed down the paternal line, and the autosomal genetic markers across all four grandparents, the grandmothers' geneticists could establish with statistical certainty whether a given person of the appropriate age was the biological child of a particular vanished couple.

The grandparentage index, developed in the 1980s by the American geneticist Mary-Claire King in collaboration with the Argentine team supporting Las Abuelas, became the scientific basis on which the recovery proceeded. By the publication of this prototype volume, one hundred and thirty grandchildren have been identified through this methodology and reunited with their biological families. The hundred-thirtieth — Estela de Carlotto's own grandson Guido — was identified on the fifth of August, 2014. He was thirty-six years old. He approached Las Abuelas himself, having begun to doubt the family story of his origins. The DNA confirmed what he had suspected. Estela met her grandson for the first time on the night of August 5, 2014, in the offices of Las Abuelas in Buenos Aires. She had been waiting thirty-six years. She was eighty-three years old.

The Thursday walk continues. Every Thursday afternoon since April 1977 — the Mothers of Plaza de Mayo began the Thursday demonstrations first, in April; the Grandmothers, formed in October, walked with them — the women have circled the Pirámide de Mayo in the central square of Buenos Aires in silence, wearing the white headscarves embroidered with the names of their disappeared children and the dates of their disappearances. The walk has now lasted over forty-eight years. The headscarves have multiplied as the names of recovered grandchildren are added to them. The Argentine state has, in the post-1983 democratic period, formally acknowledged the disappearances; the principal perpetrators have been tried; the secret detention centers have been turned into museums; the bodies thrown into the river have, in many cases, been identified through later DNA work and returned to their families. None of this restitutional work would have happened — in the form it has taken, at the pace it has taken — without the Grandmothers' insistence over the long decades that the children must be found and the truth must be acknowledged.

The wisdom of Las Abuelas is the wisdom of the carrier whose carrying is sustained by grief rather than diminished by it. The grandmothers had lost their daughters; the daughters could not be returned. What could be returned, with sufficient patience and sufficient innovation, were the grandchildren. The grandmothers converted the unrecoverable grief into the recoverable work. The conversion is the substance of their exhibit. Estela de Carlotto, in interviews given after the recovery of her grandson, has said that she does not feel that her work is done. There are, by the Abuelas' estimate, three hundred more grandchildren whose recovery is still possible. The grandmothers are now in their late eighties and nineties; many of the founding grandmothers have died. The recovery work is being carried forward, increasingly, by the grandchildren themselves — those who were recovered and who have now joined the organization as the second-generation carriers of the work.

The Pioneer Soulmate Method's third aspect — truth-seeking and the embrace of difficult dimensions — is, in this exhibit, the documented historical fact of how a small group of grandmothers refused to allow the official falsification to stand. The grandmothers did not seek revenge; they sought identification. The recovery of each grandchild was a recovery of a documentary truth that the state had constructed an elaborate machinery to conceal. The grandmothers, working at the kitchen table with a yellow legal pad and the testimony of midwives and nurses willing to speak, took apart the machinery one filing at a time. The methodology was patient. The methodology has produced one hundred and thirty restored identities, with three hundred more to come, and the development of a scientific instrument (the grandparentage index) that has subsequently been deployed in human-rights investigations in El Salvador, in the former Yugoslavia, and in the searches for the children of the Spanish stolen-babies scandal. The carrier whose carrying extended across forty-eight years has built an instrument that other carriers now use, in other countries, to do the same work.

Gaura Devi & the Chipko Women

c. 1925–1991 · Reni village, Garhwal Himalayas
The Grandmothers Who Embraced the Trees
India (Uttar Pradesh, now Uttarakhand) 26 March 1974 · 1973–present Community: the village of Reni, the Garhwal forests, the modern Indian environmental movement Transformation: the embrace of the trees and the refusal of the loggers
The Chipko movement — village women of the Garhwal Himalayas embracing the trees to prevent their felling. No verified colour portrait of Gaura Devi survives in the public domain; this period documentary image stands for the action she led at Reni. Wikimedia Commons.

On the morning of the twenty-sixth of March, 1974, a contingent of loggers contracted to a sports-equipment manufacturer in the city of Allahabad arrived at the village of Reni in the Garhwal Himalayas, in what was then the northern part of the state of Uttar Pradesh (the area would, in 2000, be separated into the new state of Uttarakhand). The loggers had a state-issued contract to cut two thousand four hundred ash trees from the forest above the village. The contract had been negotiated by the Forest Department in Lucknow with the manufacturer; the village had not been consulted; the trees that the village depended on for fuelwood, fodder, and the prevention of landslides on the steep Garhwal slopes were to be removed for the manufacture of tennis rackets and badminton equipment for the urban Indian market.

The men of Reni were not in the village on that morning. They had been called to the regional administrative center at Chamoli, several hours' walk away, by the Forest Department, ostensibly to receive overdue compensation for an unrelated land transaction. The summons was a ruse. The Forest Department, anticipating opposition from the village to the felling, had arranged to remove the male population on the day the loggers would arrive. The village would be empty, in the Department's calculation, of the bodies that might physically obstruct the felling.

"This forest is our mother's home. We will protect it with our bodies, since we cannot protect it with our voices. They have given us no voices." — Gaura Devi, attributed account of her words to the loggers at Reni, 26 March 1974

The Forest Department had not, however, accounted for the women of Reni. When the loggers arrived at the upper edge of the village, intending to walk past the houses and into the forest beyond, they were met by Gaura Devi — a widow of approximately forty-nine or fifty years of age (the exact birth year of Garhwali village women of her generation is rarely recorded), an illiterate but politically attentive head of the village's women's group (the Mahila Mangal Dal), and the de facto leader of the women's organization in the small mountain settlement. With her were twenty-seven other village women, some of whom had brought their younger daughters. The eldest among them were grandmothers; the youngest were schoolgirls.

Gaura Devi spoke to the loggers. The account of what she said exists in multiple variants in the surviving testimony, but the substance is consistent across the variants. She told the loggers that the forest was the village's mother, that the village could not survive its loss, that the women would not allow it to be cut. She told them that the men were absent because the Forest Department had arranged their absence, and that the women were now standing in for the men. She told them that the women would, if necessary, place themselves between the loggers and the trees — that the loggers would have to cut the women first if they intended to cut the trees. She used, in the colloquial Garhwali Hindi of the encounter, the verb chipakna — to cling, to embrace, to stick. The verb's noun form, chipko, became the name of the action and, eventually, of the movement that the action initiated.

The loggers, faced with twenty-eight women who appeared willing to lie down between the trees and the saws, withdrew. The chief logger consulted his foreman; the foreman consulted by radio with the Forest Department official accompanying the contingent; the official, recognizing that the political optics of an axe-and-women confrontation were not survivable in the political climate of the early Indira Gandhi years, ordered the contingent to retreat. The loggers withdrew down the slope. The contract was not executed at Reni. Word of the action reached the regional press within days. By the second week of April, the Chipko movement had begun to spread through the Garhwal villages.

The movement that followed from the Reni action is one of the most consequential environmental movements of the twentieth century. The Chipko Andolan — the Chipko movement — spread through the Garhwal Himalayas in 1974 and 1975, then into the broader Uttarakhand region, then into the neighboring Himalayan states of Himachal Pradesh and Sikkim, and eventually — in modified forms — across India. The movement's signature tactic — village women placing themselves between the loggers and the trees — was repeated in dozens of confrontations across the second half of the 1970s. The political effect was substantial. In 1980, the Indian government under Prime Minister Indira Gandhi issued a moratorium on commercial felling of green trees in the Himalayan regions of Uttar Pradesh above an altitude of one thousand meters. The moratorium has remained in force, in various forms, into the twenty-first century. The Garhwal forests that would have been cut by the contracts the movement opposed were, in the great majority of cases, preserved. The hectares of forest cover preserved by the Chipko movement and its successors number in the high tens of thousands; the prevention of consequent landslides and watershed degradation is, by the calculations of subsequent ecological assessments, substantial.

Gaura Devi did not become, in the years following the Reni action, a national or international figure on the model of Wangari Maathai. She remained in Reni village for the remainder of her life. She received, in 1986, the first Vriksha Mitra (Friend of the Tree) Award from the Government of India for her role in the Chipko movement; she received the award in a small ceremony in Dehradun, traveled to it by bus from her village, and returned to her village within forty-eight hours. She died in 1991, at approximately sixty-seven years of age, in the village she had spent her life defending. Her funeral was attended by women from twenty surrounding villages. The trees she had defended at Reni still stand.

The wisdom of Gaura Devi is the wisdom of the carrier whose single afternoon initiated a movement. Most of the carriers in this compendium have carried for decades; Gaura Devi carried for a morning. But the morning was the constitutive morning, and what she carried in those few hours — the moral authority of the village's women, the substantive refusal of the contract, the embrace of the trees as a public act — became the basis of a fifty-year political and environmental transformation that has not yet exhausted itself. The carrier's office, in this exhibit, is the office of the substantive presence at the decisive moment. She was there. She spoke. The loggers withdrew. The Chipko movement followed. The forests survived. The transformation is measurable, and the transformation has been carried, by the movement Gaura Devi began, through five subsequent decades by tens of thousands of village women whose names are, in most cases, not preserved in the documentary record, but whose collective carrying has constituted one of the most substantial environmental mobilizations of the modern Indian polity.

The Pioneer Soulmate Method's sixth aspect — diving into the deep and discovering the beginning of ignorance — is, in the Gaura Devi exhibit, the recognition that the substantive carrier may not know, at the moment of carrying, that the carrying will be constitutive. Gaura Devi did not know, on the morning of the twenty-sixth of March, 1974, that she was beginning the modern Indian environmental movement. She knew that the trees were the village's substance and that the village's women could refuse the felling. She did what was in front of her to do. The movement that followed was the consequence of the doing. The exhibit gathers her because the carrying that produces movements is, more often than the documentary record acknowledges, the carrying of village women whose immediate concern is the visible forest above their houses, and whose carrying, when the moment requires it, scales beyond the village in ways the village itself could not have predicted.

The Solar Grandmothers of Barefoot College

Founded at Tilonia, Rajasthan · solar programme from 2008
The Illiterate Elder Women Who Electrified Their Villages
India / global 2008–present Community: off-grid villages across the Global South Transformation: rural electrification by trained elder women
❦ Plate IV ❦ — image to be added. Suggested: documentary photographs of the Barefoot College solar training programme at Tilonia. Source: Wikimedia — Category:Barefoot College. (A self-hosted, rights-cleared image is preferable here.)
The Solar Grandmothers — rural elder women trained as solar engineers at the Barefoot College in Tilonia, Rajasthan.

The Barefoot College was founded in 1972 at Tilonia, in the Ajmer district of Rajasthan, by the Indian social activist Sanjit "Bunker" Roy. Its founding premise was a deliberate inversion of the development orthodoxy of its time: that the knowledge required to lift a village out of poverty did not need to be imported from the city or the university, but could be cultivated among the village's own people — including, and especially, among those whom the formal economy had written off as unteachable. The college took as its faculty the rural poor themselves: water-diviners, midwives, builders, and artisans whose competence had no certificate to attest to it. The institution's name is its argument. The barefoot are not the objects of development. They are its engineers.

The programme for which the college became internationally known began to take its mature form in the years around 2008, when the college concentrated its solar-electrification training on a specific and counter-intuitive population: illiterate and semi-literate grandmothers from the most under-resourced villages on earth. The logic was practical and hard-won. Young men, trained as technicians, tended to migrate to the cities in search of wages once they held a marketable skill; the training left with them. Grandmothers did not migrate. They were rooted in their villages by family, by status, and by a lifetime of belonging. A grandmother trained as a solar engineer would return to her village and stay — and the village would keep the light.

"Why grandmothers? Because they don't leave. Train a young man and he goes to the city. Train a grandmother and she lights the village — and she is still there in twenty years." — the operating principle of the Barefoot solar programme, as Bunker Roy has often summarized it

The training itself is conducted at Tilonia in courses of roughly six months. The women who attend are drawn, increasingly, not only from across India but from villages across Africa, Latin America, the Pacific, and the least-electrified regions of Asia, brought to Rajasthan under the auspices of the Indian government's technical-cooperation programmes. They arrive sharing, in many cases, no common spoken language with their instructors or with one another. This would seem an insuperable obstacle to technical instruction. It is not. The college developed a pedagogy built on color-coding, repetition, sign language, and the physical handling of components — a curriculum that bypasses literacy entirely. A woman who cannot read learns to assemble, install, repair, and maintain a solar lighting system, a charge controller, and the circuitry that connects them, by doing it with her hands until the doing is certain.

The grandmother returns to her village a solar engineer. She installs the panels on the roofs. She fits the lamps. She maintains a workshop and trains the next woman. The household that had burned kerosene — expensive, dim, dangerous to the lungs and to the house — has electric light. The child who had no light to study by has light. The clinic that closed at dusk stays open. By the programme's own accounting, the model has produced verified electrification across well over two thousand villages in dozens of countries, achieved not by an engineering firm or an aid agency but by elder women who, in the estimation of every institution that had ever measured them, possessed no qualification of any kind.

The Solar Grandmothers belong in this compendium because their carrying is the carrying of the community across the threshold of darkness, and because the methodology by which they were enabled to carry is the carrier methodology in its purest institutional form. The Barefoot College did not carry the villages. It carried the grandmothers — recognized in them a substantive capacity that no one else had recognized, sustained them through the bounded developmental ground of the six-month course, and returned them to do the carrying themselves. The light in the village is the measurable transformation. The grandmother who installed it, and who will still be there to repair it long after any visiting technician would have gone, is the carrier the methodology describes.

Grace Lee Boggs

1915–2015 · Born Providence, Rhode Island · Died Detroit
The Philosopher of the Next American Revolution
United States (Detroit) 1950s–2015 Community: the city of Detroit and the American radical tradition Transformation: the re-imagining of revolution as community-building
Grace Lee Boggs, philosopher and activist of Detroit. Wikimedia Commons. (Filename unverified from the build environment — confirm against the live render.)

Grace Lee was born in Providence, Rhode Island, in 1915, the daughter of Chinese immigrants; her father ran a restaurant in New York City. She took a degree from Barnard and then, in 1940, a doctorate in philosophy from Bryn Mawr College — at a moment when a Chinese-American woman with a PhD in philosophy could not, in the America of her time, find a university willing to hire her. The closed door turned her toward the work that would occupy the next seventy-five years. She moved to Chicago, took a low-paying job, lived in a rat-infested basement, and entered the tenants'-rights organizing of the Black neighborhoods around her. The philosopher of Hegel and Mead became, by way of the housing struggle, a revolutionary.

In 1953 she moved to Detroit and married James Boggs, an African-American autoworker, writer, and radical activist. The marriage was also a partnership of thought; for four decades, until James's death in 1993, the two wrote and organized together at the center of Detroit's Black radical tradition. Grace, the Chinese-American intellectual, became so thoroughly a part of that tradition that an FBI file on her activities reportedly speculated that she might be Afro-Chinese. She had, in the deepest sense, been carried into a community not her own by birth, and had then turned and carried it in return.

"The most radical thing I ever did was to stay put." — Grace Lee Boggs

The transformation Boggs carried was, in its early decades, the conventional transformation of the militant: she passed through Marxism, through the labor movement, through the Black Power movement, through every major current of the twentieth-century American left. But the deeper and more original transformation came later, and it came out of Detroit's collapse. As the automobile industry abandoned the city and Detroit hollowed into one of the starkest landscapes of American deindustrialization, Boggs concluded that the revolution she had spent her life pursuing had been wrongly conceived. Revolution was not the seizure of the state. It was not the next strike or the next uprising. It was, she came to argue, the patient work of growing a new kind of human being and a new kind of community in the ruins the old order had left behind — what she called, in her later vocabulary, "the next American revolution."

She put the philosophy to work in the concrete. In 1992 she co-founded Detroit Summer, an intergenerational, multiracial youth program that planted community gardens in the city's vacant lots, rehabilitated houses, and painted murals — work that married the elder's memory to the young person's energy, and that treated the devastated city not as a problem to be fled but as ground in which something new could be grown. The community garden in the vacant lot is the Boggs methodology made visible: you do not wait for the revolution; you plant the garden, and the planting is the revolution. She and James founded what became the James and Grace Lee Boggs Center to Nurture Community Leadership, which carries the work still.

Boggs wrote throughout. Her memoir Living for Change (1998) and her late testament The Next American Revolution: Sustainable Activism for the Twenty-First Century (2011), published when she was ninety-five, articulated a vision of social change rooted in place, in relationship, and in the long patience of the grandmother rather than the impatience of the militant. She died at her Detroit home on the fifth of October, 2015, at the age of one hundred. The gardens she helped plant continue. The young people she carried are now themselves the elders carrying others. The philosopher who could not get a university job in 1940 transformed, across a century, the way a generation of American activists understood what a revolution is for.

Las Patronas of Guadalupe

From 1995 · La Patrona, Amatlán de los Reyes, Veracruz
The Women Who Feed the Migrants on the Train
Mexico (Veracruz) 1995–present Community: Central American migrants riding La Bestia northward Transformation: the daily provision of food and water to those in transit
❦ Plate VI ❦ — image to be added. Suggested: documentary photographs of Las Patronas handing food to migrants aboard La Bestia. Source: Wikimedia — Category:Las Patronas. (A self-hosted, rights-cleared press image is preferable here.)
Las Patronas — the women of La Patrona, Veracruz, passing bagged food and bottled water to migrants on the moving freight train.

The freight train that crosses Mexico from the southern border toward the north is called, by the Central American migrants who ride it, La Bestia — the Beast — and also el tren de la muerte, the train of death. Migrants fleeing the violence and poverty of Honduras, Guatemala, and El Salvador climb onto its roofs and cling to the spaces between its cars for the length of Mexico, without ticket, without seat, without water. They fall asleep and fall under the wheels. They are robbed and extorted and thrown from the moving train. The journey takes days, and along most of its length there is no one to help.

In the state of Veracruz, the train passes the village of La Patrona, in the municipality of Amatlán de los Reyes. It was there, in 1995, that the transformation began — and it began with an ordinary act. Two sisters of the Romero Vázquez family had gone to buy bread and milk and were walking home along the tracks when a train passed slowly and the migrants riding it called down to them, hungry, asking for the food in their arms. The sisters gave it. They went home and told their mother, Leonila Vázquez Alvízar, what had happened. The family decided that they would do it again — that they would cook, and carry the food to the tracks, and give it to whoever passed. They have done it, now, for three decades.

"We cannot solve the migrant's whole journey. We can make sure that, as he passes our village, he is not hungry. That much is in our hands, and so we do it." — the ethic of Las Patronas, as the women have described their work

The work is daily and physical. The women — Las Patronas, named for their village — rise early and cook rice and beans in great quantities, fill plastic bags so they can be caught from a moving train, fill bottles with water, and carry the food to the trackside. When the train comes, they stand along the line and hand or toss the bags up to the migrants who lean down from the cars with their arms out. A bag of rice, a bottle of water, a few seconds of contact at the speed of a freight train. Multiply it across thirty years and the count of those fed reaches into the high hundreds of thousands. The group has expanded its work over the decades to include a shelter and aid for migrants who stop, and the founding women — many now in their fifties, sixties, and beyond — have trained their daughters and neighbors to continue it.

Las Patronas have been recognized within Mexico as a moral landmark; the collective received Mexico's National Human Rights Prize in 2013. But the recognition is incidental to the substance. The substance is the bag of rice handed up to the moving train at dawn, every day, for thirty years, to strangers the women will never see again and who can give them nothing in return. This is the grandmother's carrying in its starkest form: the carried does not stay, cannot reciprocate, often cannot even be seen clearly in the seconds of passing. The recognition runs one direction only. The women feed them anyway. The exhibit gathers Las Patronas because their carrying answers, more plainly than any argument could, the question of what the carrier owes the carried: she owes presence at the moment of need, and she owes it whether or not the carried can ever know her name.

Maggie Kuhn & the Gray Panthers

1905–1995 · Founded the movement 1970
The Grandmother Who Made Old Age a Political Force
United States 1970–present Community: the old, the young, and the alliance between them Transformation: the politics of age, against ageism and for justice
Maggie Kuhn, founder of the Gray Panthers. Wikimedia Commons. (Filename unverified from the build environment — confirm against the live render.)

Margaret Kuhn was born in 1905 and spent her working life in the service of the Presbyterian Church, in social-justice and education work she pursued with energy and conviction for decades. In 1970, on her sixty-fifth birthday, she was compelled to retire — not because she wished to, and not because her capacities had failed, but because the institution's policy required it. She was, by the arithmetic of the retirement age, finished. The forced ending was meant to remove her from public life. It did the opposite. It began the most consequential phase of it.

Kuhn gathered five friends, all of them women facing the same mandatory retirement, and they began to meet to discuss what they shared: not the supposed serenity of old age, but its sudden powerlessness — the loss of role, of income, of standing, of voice, the consignment to invisibility that American society imposed on its elders. The group they formed took the deliberately unsentimental view that old age was not a private misfortune to be borne quietly but a political condition to be organized around. A television host, it is said, dubbed the group the Gray Panthers, after the Black Panthers; the name stuck, and Kuhn embraced it.

"Speak your mind, even if your voice shakes." — Maggie Kuhn

The Gray Panthers refused, from the start, the premise that the movement was about the old alone. Kuhn insisted on intergenerational alliance — that the interests of the old and the young were not opposed but joined, and that the same society that warehoused its elders in nursing homes also failed its young, and that the two generations had common cause against the structures that diminished them both. The movement took up nursing-home reform, exposing the conditions in institutions where the old were neglected and abused. It fought age discrimination in employment. It campaigned for healthcare, against the Vietnam War, and for a politics that treated the whole human lifespan as a continuity rather than a series of disposable phases.

Kuhn herself became a national figure — sharp, funny, unintimidated, entirely willing to say on television what the powerful preferred unsaid. She made her own age her instrument. She described herself and her peers not as senior citizens, a euphemism she despised, but as elders with a role to play precisely because they were old: free of the careerist's caution, with nothing left to lose and a lifetime of knowledge to spend. The grandmother, in Kuhn's formulation, was not society's dependent but its conscience — the one positioned, by age and by freedom from ambition, to tell the truth.

She led the movement until her death in 1995, at the age of eighty-nine. The Gray Panthers continue as an organization, and the broader transformation Kuhn carried — the reconception of old age as a site of political agency rather than passive decline, and the dismantling of the assumption that a person past sixty-five has nothing further to contribute — has outlived her by decades. The exhibit gathers her because she took the very condition that was meant to silence her, the forced retirement that was meant to end her, and made it the ground of her carrying. She carried a generation of elders out of invisibility, and she did it by refusing, with her voice shaking or not, to go quiet.

Doris "Granny D" Haddock

1910–2010 · New Hampshire
The Grandmother Who Walked Across America
United States 1999–2000 (the walk); active to her death in 2010 Community: the American electorate and the cause of clean government Transformation: campaign-finance reform carried on foot, coast to coast
Doris "Granny D" Haddock, who walked across the United States for campaign-finance reform in her ninetieth year. Wikimedia Commons. (Filename unverified from the build environment — confirm against the live render.)

Doris Haddock was born in 1910 in Laconia, New Hampshire. She lived the long, mostly private life of a New England woman of her generation — a marriage of many decades, work, children, the death of her husband, the ordinary accumulation of years. She was, at the close of the century, an eighty-eight-year-old widow with arthritis and emphysema. She was not a public figure. She had no organization behind her, no office, no platform. What she had was an objection — to the corruption of American democracy by private money — and a conviction that an old woman with nothing left to lose was exactly the right person to register it in the most public way she could conceive.

What she conceived was a walk. In 1999, at the age of eighty-nine, Doris Haddock set out from Los Angeles to walk across the United States to Washington, D.C., in support of campaign-finance reform — the cause then associated with the McCain-Feingold legislation. She walked roughly ten miles a day. She walked through the Mojave Desert. She walked through the cold of a continental winter, at one point crossing a snowbound stretch on skis when walking became impossible. She walked for fourteen months and some three thousand miles, and she arrived on the steps of the United States Capitol in February 2000, accompanied by supporters who had joined her along the way, in the month of her ninetieth birthday.

"Democracy is not something we have. It is something we do." — Doris "Granny D" Haddock

The walk was, in one sense, a single old woman moving slowly across a vast country. In another sense it was an argument that could not be ignored precisely because of who was making it. A lobbyist's millions could be dismissed as interest; a senator's speech could be dismissed as politics; but a ninety-year-old woman walking through the desert in winter for no personal gain whatsoever was an act of pure civic witness that the press could not look away from and that ordinary Americans recognized instantly. Her age was not incidental to the message. It was the message. She had no future to advance and no fortune to make. She walked because the thing was right, and the spectacle of disinterested conviction in a frail and determined body did what no amount of advocacy spending could have done.

She did not stop at the Capitol. In the decade that remained to her, Granny D continued to campaign for clean government and voter participation. In 2004, at the age of ninety-four, she ran for the United States Senate from New Hampshire — not because she expected to win, but because the seat would otherwise have gone uncontested, and an uncontested seat was, to her, a small surrender of democracy that she was unwilling to permit. She died in 2010, at the age of one hundred. The campaign-finance battle she walked for has not been won; the money in American politics has, if anything, grown. But the figure of the grandmother walking across the continent for an idea entered the country's civic imagination and remains there. The exhibit gathers her because she demonstrates that the carrier's instrument can be nothing more than her own body, moving slowly in the right direction, for as long as it takes — and that this, witnessed, can carry a whole nation's attention to a truth it preferred to ignore.

The International Council of Thirteen Indigenous Grandmothers

The Thirteen Indigenous Grandmothers — elders convened from indigenous traditions across the Americas, Africa, Asia, and the Arctic.

In the autumn of 2004, thirteen elder women — grandmothers in the literal and the honorific sense — gathered at the Menla Mountain Retreat in the Catskills of New York, drawn from indigenous traditions on every inhabited continent. They came from the Amazon basin and the North American plains, from the Arctic, from Africa, from the Himalayas, from Central America and Oaxaca. They had never before assembled as a body. They came in response to a shared sense, arrived at independently across their traditions, that the world had entered a time of danger — to the earth, to indigenous lifeways, to the spiritual knowledge that the elder generations carried and that was passing out of the world as the elders died and the young were drawn away. They formed themselves into the International Council of Thirteen Indigenous Grandmothers and vowed to work together for the protection of that knowledge and of the planet that had given rise to it.

The Council's founding was, in its structure, an enactment of the carrier methodology at the scale of the whole earth. Each of the thirteen was already a carrier within her own people — a keeper of medicine, of ceremony, of language, of ecological knowledge accumulated across uncounted generations. What the Council did was to gather these carriers into a single body whose carrying was no longer confined to a single people but addressed to the world. The grandmothers carried, into rooms that had never before admitted them, a form of knowledge that the modern world had spent five centuries dismissing as superstition and was only now, in its ecological crisis, beginning to suspect it could not survive without.

"We are the grandmothers. We have come together to pray, to teach, and to heal — for our children, and for the next seven generations." — the founding spirit of the Council of Thirteen Indigenous Grandmothers

The phrase "the next seven generations" — the principle, widespread across indigenous American traditions, that a decision must be weighed by its consequences seven generations into the future — became the Council's signature. It names precisely what distinguishes the grandmother's timescale from the modern world's. The quarterly report, the electoral cycle, the news day: these are the horizons within which industrial society makes its decisions. The grandmother's horizon is the great-great-great-great-great-grandchild not yet born. The Council carried that horizon into United Nations forums, into ecological and interfaith gatherings, into the documentary record and the public conversation, insisting that the question always be asked: what will this do to the ones who come seven generations after us?

The Council has traveled to one another's homelands, held public gatherings, prayed at sites sacred to the world's traditions, and brought indigenous voice and ceremony into spaces of global power from which they had been historically excluded. Their work was documented in the film For the Next 7 Generations (2009). As founding members have aged and died across the two decades since 2004, the Council has faced the question every carrier-body must eventually face — how the carrying continues when the carriers are gone — and has answered it as grandmothers answer it: by teaching, so that the knowledge passes to those who will carry it next. The exhibit gathers the Council because it makes explicit what the whole compendium argues. The grandmother carries the community toward a destination the community cannot yet see. The Council names that destination plainly: the world that will exist seven generations from now, and the obligation of the living to carry it well.

Anna Mary Robertson Moses

1860–1961 · "Grandma Moses" · Greenwich, New York
The Grandmother Who Began at Seventy-Eight
United States c. 1938–1961 Community: American popular culture and the standing of folk art Transformation: the late-life remaking of a nation's idea of who may be an artist
Anna Mary Robertson "Grandma" Moses, who took up painting in her late seventies. Wikimedia Commons. (Filename unverified from the build environment — confirm against the live render.)

Anna Mary Robertson was born in 1860 in Greenwich, New York, in the farm country east of the Hudson. She lived, for nearly eight decades, the life that history does not record: a farm girl who went into service as a hired girl at twelve, who married a farm worker, who bore ten children of whom five survived, who worked the land in New York and Virginia, who was widowed, who grew old. She had made things with her hands all her life — embroidery in worsted yarn, the decorative needlework that was the permitted art of a farm woman. There was nothing in this life to suggest that her name would become, before she died, one of the most recognized in the United States.

In her late seventies, arthritis made the needle impossible to hold. Rather than surrender the making of images altogether, she took up a brush — the brush being easier on the stiffened hands than the needle — and began to paint. She painted what she knew: the rural scenes of her long life, the sugaring-off in the maple woods, the harvest, the first snow, the farm in every season, painted from memory in bright unschooled color with no regard for the perspective or technique that the academies taught. She gave the paintings away, or sold them for a few dollars at the county fair and the local drugstore, where they hung among the jams and the home preserves.

"Life is what we make it, always has been, always will be." — Grandma Moses

In 1938 a New York collector named Louis Caldor, passing through the village, saw the paintings in the drugstore window, bought them all, and sought out the painter. Within a few years the farm widow's pictures had been shown in New York, and the public response was immediate and enormous. "Grandma Moses" — the name the press gave her — became a national phenomenon. Her paintings were reproduced on greeting cards and curtains and dishware; they entered the most fashionable galleries and the most ordinary American homes at once. She was featured in the great magazines, interviewed, photographed, celebrated. She kept painting — well over a thousand works across the last two decades of her life, some of them after she had passed one hundred.

The transformation she carried was not of a village or a forest but of a nation's imagination of itself, and specifically of its idea of who is permitted to be an artist and when. Before Grandma Moses, the American picture of the artist was the young bohemian, the trained professional, the person who had given a life to the craft. She demolished it simply by existing: a great-grandmother with no training who began at seventy-eight and became one of the most beloved painters the country had produced. Her hundredth birthday, in 1960, was proclaimed "Grandma Moses Day" by the Governor of New York. She died the following year, in 1961, at the age of one hundred and one.

The exhibit gathers her because she carries a transformation the other grandmothers do not — not of a community's material conditions but of its sense of the possible. She is the proof, lodged permanently in the popular memory of an entire country, that the last chapter of a long and unremarked life can be the one that changes everything, and that the faculty for making something new is not the property of the young. Every person who has ever said "it is too late for me to begin" has been answered, whether they know it or not, by a farm widow who picked up a brush at seventy-eight because her hands could no longer hold the needle.

Tulasi Gowda was born around 1944 into the Halakki Vokkaliga community, an indigenous people of the Uttara Kannada district of Karnataka, in the forested hills of India's Western Ghats. Her circumstances were the circumstances of the rural indigenous poor at their starkest. Her father died when she was a small child; she never attended a school; she worked, from early childhood, as a daily-wage laborer alongside her mother. She could not read or write, and she never learned to. By every metric the modern state uses to measure a person's prospects, she had none.

What she had instead was the forest, and a knowledge of it so deep and so exact that it would eventually astonish the trained foresters of the state. She knew the trees of the Western Ghats — their species, their seasons, their seeds, the precise conditions under which a given seed would germinate and a given seedling would take root and survive. She entered the work of the Karnataka Forest Department, at first as a daily-wage laborer in its nurseries, and across the decades that followed she planted and nurtured trees on a scale that beggars ordinary accounting. The figure most often cited, drawn from her own and the department's records, runs to some tens of thousands of trees raised across her lifetime — and beyond the number, a survival rate and a quality of care that came from a knowledge no forestry degree had conferred.

"She knows the mother tree of every species — which seed will grow, and which will not. The forest department came to her to learn it." — the regional account of Tulasi Gowda's knowledge, which earned her the name "Encyclopedia of the Forest"

In the Kannada-speaking region she became known as the "Encyclopedia of the Forest" — vriksha matheya, the one who knows the trees as a mother knows her children. The honorific was not sentimental. It named a documented fact: that a woman who could not read held, in her memory and her hands, a working knowledge of a complex forest ecology that the formally trained could not match, and that they came to her to learn. She was the keeper, for her people and her place, of an ecological literacy that the wider society had nearly forgotten it needed.

In 2021 the Government of India awarded Tulasi Gowda the Padma Shri, one of the nation's highest civilian honors. The photographs of her receiving it became, briefly, famous: an elderly Halakki woman in traditional dress, barefoot, standing among the suited dignitaries of the capital, entirely composed. The image carried the whole argument of this compendium in a single frame — the carrier whom every institution had overlooked, honored at last for a transformation she had been quietly enacting, tree by tree, for longer than most of the room had been alive. She died in 2024, at around eighty years of age, in the district where she had spent her life. The forests she planted stand. The exhibit gathers her because her carrying is the carrying of the forest itself across the threshold of its own destruction, performed across a lifetime by an illiterate indigenous woman whose knowledge the modern world dismissed until the moment it needed to be saved by it.

Ela Bhatt & SEWA

1933–2022 · Founded SEWA 1972 · Ahmedabad, Gujarat
The Gentle Revolutionary of the Self-Employed
India (Gujarat) 1972–present Community: the self-employed women workers of the informal economy Transformation: the organization of the unorganized into a movement millions strong
Ela Bhatt, founder of the Self-Employed Women's Association (SEWA). Wikimedia Commons. (Filename unverified from the build environment — confirm against the live render.)

Ela Bhatt was born in 1933 in Ahmedabad, in the western Indian state of Gujarat, and trained as a lawyer. She came of age in the afterglow of the Indian independence movement and within the living tradition of Gandhi, whose principles of nonviolence, self-reliance, and dignified labor would shape every decision of her working life. She joined the legal department of the Textile Labour Association — the union founded under Gandhi's influence — and there encountered the women who would become her life's work: not the factory workers the union represented, but the vast, invisible population of women who labored outside any factory, any contract, any law.

These were the self-employed women of the informal economy — the street vendors, the home-based garment workers, the head-loaders and cart-pullers, the rag-pickers, the women who rolled cigarettes and stitched at home for piece-rates that no minimum wage protected. They were, in the categories of the formal economy and the conventional labor movement alike, invisible: not employees, so no employer could be held to account for them; not organized, so no union spoke for them; not counted, so no policy reached them. They were the majority of India's working women and they had no standing of any kind. In 1972, Ela Bhatt founded the Self-Employed Women's Association — SEWA — to give them one.

"When women move forward, the family moves, the village moves, the nation moves." — a principle long associated with Ela Bhatt and SEWA

SEWA was a labor union of the unorganized — a thing the labor movement had said could not exist, because its members had no single employer to strike against. Bhatt's insight was that the union model could be turned toward a different purpose: not collective bargaining against a boss, but collective strength turned to every dimension of the self-employed woman's life. SEWA organized its members into cooperatives. It founded its own bank — the SEWA Cooperative Bank — owned by and lending to women whom every other bank considered unbankable, so that a vegetable vendor need not surrender her day's profit to a moneylender. It provided healthcare, childcare, insurance, legal aid, and training. It fought, in the courts and the legislatures and the streets, for the legal recognition of the informal worker as a worker.

Across the half-century since its founding, SEWA has grown into one of the largest organizations of informal-sector workers in the world, its membership numbering in the millions of women across India. Its model has been studied and replicated internationally, and it has shaped the way global institutions understand the informal economy — no longer as a marginal remnant destined to disappear, but as the actual condition of most of the world's workers, requiring its own forms of organization and protection. Bhatt herself became a figure of international moral authority; she was among the founding members of The Elders, the group convened by Nelson Mandela. She was known, across a life of patient and unwavering work, as "the gentle revolutionary" — the one who transformed the conditions of millions without ever raising her voice or abandoning the Gandhian commitment to nonviolence and dignity.

She died in 2022, at the age of eighty-nine. SEWA continues, millions strong. The exhibit gathers her because she carried the most invisible workers in one of the world's largest economies into visibility, organization, and power — and because her carrying demonstrates that the grandmother's methods (patience, presence, the long unspectacular labor of building an institution that will outlast her) can move not a village but a multitude.

The Mothers of Soacha

From 2008 · Soacha, greater Bogotá, Colombia
The Mothers Who Named the False Positives
Colombia 2008–present Community: the families of the murdered young men of Soacha Transformation: the exposure and indictment of the falsos positivos
❦ Plate XIII ❦ — image to be added. Suggested: documentary photographs of the Madres de Soacha / MAFAPO holding portraits of their sons. Source: Wikimedia Commons search. (A self-hosted, rights-cleared press image is preferable here.)
The Mothers of Soacha — mothers and grandmothers holding the photographs of the young men killed and falsely presented as guerrillas.

Soacha is a poor, dense municipality on the southern edge of Bogotá, the place where the Colombian capital's displaced and impoverished have settled at its margins. In 2008 the families of Soacha began to notice that their young men were disappearing — sons and grandsons, many of them poor, some with disabilities, lured from the neighborhood with the promise of work. Weeks later, some of them were found dead, hundreds of kilometers away, in distant regions of the country, buried in unmarked graves and recorded by the Colombian army as guerrilla combatants killed in battle.

They were not guerrillas. They were unemployed young men from a Bogotá slum who had been recruited under false pretenses, transported across the country, murdered, dressed in combat fatigues, and presented by army units as enemy dead — because the military's internal incentive structure rewarded body counts, and a dead "guerrilla" earned commendations, leave, and promotion regardless of who the dead man actually was. The killings came to be known, in the chillingly bureaucratic euphemism of the scandal, as the falsos positivos — the false positives. They were among the gravest atrocities of Colombia's long internal conflict, and they were committed not against the enemy but against the country's own poorest citizens, for career advantage.

"They took our sons and called them combatants. We had their faces, their names, their birthdays. We would not let the lie stand." — the witness of the Mothers of Soacha

The transformation was carried by the mothers and grandmothers of the murdered men. They did exactly what the perpetrators had counted on the poor being unable to do: they refused the official story, and they organized to take it apart. They gathered the documentation — the photographs, the birth certificates, the records of when their sons had vanished and when and where the army claimed to have killed them in combat, distances and timelines that could not be reconciled. They formed an association, MAFAPO (Madres de Falsos Positivos), and they carried the case into the press, into the Colombian courts, and into the international human-rights system, across years in which they were dismissed, threatened, and told to be silent.

They were not silent. Their persistence forced the falsos positivos from a deniable rumor into one of the central reckonings of the Colombian conflict. Colombia's transitional-justice tribunal, established after the 2016 peace accord, took up the case and put the documented scale of the killings — far larger than Soacha alone, reaching into the thousands across the country and the years — into the official record, with senior officers among those called to account. None of this acknowledgment, none of this accountability, would have come at the pace or the scale it did without the mothers of Soacha who would not accept that their sons had been guerrillas, and who carried the truth of who they were against the full weight of a state that had murdered them and lied.

The exhibit gathers the Mothers of Soacha alongside the Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo because the two collectives are sisters across a continent and a generation: grandmothers and mothers who converted the unbearable grief of a disappeared or murdered child into the patient, documentary, decades-long work of forcing a state to admit what it had done. The carried, here, are the dead, who cannot be returned. What the carrying recovers is their names — the restoration of the truth that they were sons, not combatants, and that their killing was a crime. That recovery, against a state's machinery of falsification, is the same carrying the third aspect of the method describes: the truth-seeking that embraces the most difficult dimension of all, and will not let the lie stand.

— Exhibit XIV —

Setsuko Thurlow

b. 1932 · Survivor of Hiroshima · Hiroshima & Toronto
The Grandmother Against the Bomb
Japan / Canada 1945 (the bombing); seven decades of campaigning to the present Community: humanity, under the shadow of nuclear weapons Transformation: the moral case for nuclear abolition, carried to a UN treaty
Setsuko Thurlow, Hiroshima survivor and nuclear-abolition campaigner, who accepted the 2017 Nobel Peace Prize on behalf of ICAN. Wikimedia Commons. (Filename unverified from the build environment — confirm against the live render.)

Setsuko Nakamura was thirteen years old on the morning of the sixth of August, 1945, when the United States detonated an atomic bomb over Hiroshima. She was a schoolgirl, mobilized with her classmates for the war effort, in a building roughly a mile and a half from the point above which the bomb exploded. The blast collapsed the building on top of her. She came to in the dark, buried, hearing the voices of her classmates around her crying for their mothers and for water, and then the voices going silent one by one. A man's hand reached her and a voice told her not to give up, to keep pushing toward the light. She crawled out. Most of the girls in that building did not. She emerged into a city that had become, in a single flash, a landscape of the dead and the dying — among them members of her own family. She was thirteen.

She survived. She became, in the vocabulary that the survivors of the atomic bombings made necessary, a hibakusha — a bomb-affected person — and she resolved that the world would not be permitted to forget or to repeat what had been done to her city. She emigrated to Canada, married, raised a family, and settled in Toronto, and from there she began the work that would occupy the rest of her long life: bearing witness. She told the story of that morning, again and again, across seven decades, to audiences in classrooms and conference halls and the chambers of the United Nations — refusing the comfort of silence, reliving the worst hours of her life in public over and over, because she had concluded that her testimony was a weapon against the weapon, and the only one she had.

"I have been waiting seventy-three years for this. Let this be the beginning of the end of nuclear weapons." — Setsuko Thurlow, on the adoption of the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons

Her witness became one of the moral engines of the movement that produced, in 2017, the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons — the first treaty to ban nuclear weapons outright under international law. Thurlow had become a leading voice of the International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons (ICAN), the coalition whose advocacy drove the treaty's adoption. When ICAN was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize that year, it chose Setsuko Thurlow, the Hiroshima grandmother, to accept it alongside the campaign's director and to deliver the Nobel lecture. She stood in Oslo, eighty-five years old, and told the world what she had seen as a thirteen-year-old in the ruins, and made of that telling an argument that the possession of these weapons by anyone, ever, is an abomination that humanity must abolish.

The weapons have not been abolished; the nuclear-armed states have not signed the treaty. Thurlow's carrying, like Granny D's, is of a transformation not yet complete. But she carried the moral fact of Hiroshima — not the statistic but the lived reality of it, held in the body of a survivor — into the center of international law, and made it impossible for the world to claim it did not know. The exhibit gathers her as the compendium's last carrier because her work names the largest community any grandmother in this volume has carried: not a village, not a city, not a nation, but the whole of humanity under the shadow of its own capacity for annihilation. She was buried alive at thirteen and crawled toward the light because a voice told her not to give up. She has spent the eight decades since being that voice for everyone else.

Closing Note

Fourteen exhibits at canonical depth; the framing essay that distinguishes the grandmother register from the horse register; the index that organizes the whole. The volume is enough to see the shape of the second compendium. The Reverie of Horses gathers mythological carriers; the Reverie of Grandmothers gathers historical carriers; the two volumes together establish that the carrier-and-carried pattern operates both in the imaginal register that the human imagination has preserved across millennia and in the documentary register that the human record has preserved across the most recent fifty years. The pattern is the pattern. The carriers are the carriers. The difference between Buraq and Wangari Maathai is the difference between the singular night of cosmic ascent and the fifty-one million trees planted by women's hands across thirty-four years. Both are substantive. Both are required.

My instinct is to leave the count open. The horses are finite because the mythological traditions are finite; the grandmothers are not. Every village has at least one. Every town has more. The library is, in this register, structurally infinite, and the patient gathering is structurally without end. That, too, is the methodology.
